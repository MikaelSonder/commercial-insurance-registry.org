Commercial insurance in China is the business service line through which companies identify, structure, place and administer risk transfer for property, business interruption, general and product liability, cyber, professional indemnity, directors’ and officers’ liability, construction, marine, cargo, credit, surety and other corporate exposures. It is integral to risk management for Chinese manufacturers, exporters, technology companies, construction groups, logistics operators, real-estate developers, energy businesses and multinational companies with mainland Chinese operations.

The National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA) and its local bureaus exercise unified supervision of China’s insurance and reinsurance sector. The Insurance Law of the People’s Republic of China is the core legal framework; it regulates insurance activities, protects parties’ lawful interests and requires insurance companies, reinsurers, agencies and brokers operating in China to hold the relevant approval or licence. NFRA controls market entry, business scope, branch approvals, governance, risk management, internal controls, disclosure and solvency supervision.[web:420][web:422][web:412]

China’s insurance distribution structure distinguishes insurer-side agents from policyholder-side brokers. An insurance broker is an entity that, in the interest of the insurance applicant, provides intermediary services between applicant and insurer for the conclusion of an insurance contract and receives commission in accordance with law. An insurance agent acts under an authorisation agreement with an insurer and transacts insurance business on the insurer’s behalf. Agencies and brokers require the corresponding NFRA insurance agency or broker licence.[web:415][web:413][web:420]

For international businesses, commercial insurance in China is a local market-access, licensing, contract-language and regulatory-compliance exercise — not an extension of EU/EEA passporting. Unlicensed foreign insurers are not permitted to conduct insurance business in China. Foreign investors may establish wholly foreign-owned insurers, joint ventures or branches subject to applicable rules, while foreign reinsurers may accept cessions from Chinese cedents under the registration and financial-strength conditions of the China Reinsurance Registration System. As of September 2026, NFRA has released a draft revision of the Insurance Law for public consultation; the currently effective law and applicable NFRA rules remain the operative framework pending legislative change.[web:422][web:420][web:423]

Object Definition

Commercial insurance in China is the corporate risk-transfer function through which a business assesses exposures and obtains insurance protection for defined financial consequences of property damage, business interruption, liability, professional error, cyber events, product failure, construction risk, marine and cargo losses and other business contingencies. It includes direct placement with a licensed insurer or placement through a licensed broker or agency, underwriting submissions, policy negotiation, certificates, claims notification, loss mitigation and renewal planning.

Scope

The Registry Object covers the practical architecture of commercial insurance placement and administration for organisations operating in or from mainland China. It focuses on voluntary corporate and industrial insurance, insurer/broker/agency roles, NFRA supervision and licensing, policy contract law, claims, renewal, compulsory insurance, reinsurance and multinational programme relevance.

Purpose

The purpose of commercial insurance is to transfer defined financial exposure from the corporate policyholder to a licensed insurer on terms appropriate to the company’s operations, assets, contracts, liabilities, supply chains, financing and risk appetite. It supports continuity, tendering, project delivery, export activity, lender requirements and claims recovery, while leaving underlying operating and contractual risk with the business.

PurposeTo identify, assess and transfer material corporate risk through insurance cover appropriate to the policyholder’s activities, assets, liabilities, contracts and China-related footprint.
Business ValueStructured placement can protect liquidity and assets, support recovery after loss, satisfy finance and client requirements, and provide a defined insurer-backed claims route.

Primary Outcome

The primary outcome of a Chinese commercial insurance engagement is a bound policy or coordinated programme identifying the policyholder, insured entities, covered risks, limits, deductibles, exclusions, conditions, territory, policy period and claims process. It is a defined allocation of risk rather than unlimited protection against all business loss, contractual liability, regulatory exposure or operational delay.

Primary OutcomeA bound commercial insurance policy or programme documenting the agreed corporate risk-transfer structure.
Decision BoundaryA broker, agency or insurer may support placement within its authorised role, but the policyholder remains responsible for underwriting information, coverage selection, local compliance and retained-risk decisions.
Implementation StepClaims, policy endorsements, certificate requests, entity additions, regulatory reporting and renewal negotiations follow the original placement.

Commercial insurance in China is used by businesses with material industrial, property, construction, professional, liability, transport, cyber, export, regulatory or contractual exposure. It is particularly relevant to companies with Chinese production sites, supply chains, distribution networks, product liability, local employees, infrastructure assets or multinational insurance requirements.

Country Characteristics

China’s commercial insurance environment is characterised by unified NFRA supervision, a domestic licensing requirement for insurers and intermediaries, a clear statutory distinction between applicant-side brokers and insurer-side agents, and strong local operational requirements for foreign insurers. China does not recognise an EU/EEA-type insurance passport. The legal and commercial focus is therefore on Chinese market entry, local licensing, policy issuance, claims capability and compliance with NFRA rules.[web:420][web:422][web:415]

Operational CultureRelationship- and documentation-led, with local insurer, broker and agency capability important for corporate placement. State, local and sectoral regulatory expectations should be considered alongside commercial underwriting.
Institutional StructureNFRA and its local bureaus exercise unified supervision over insurers, reinsurers, insurance asset management companies, agents and brokers, including market entry, business scope, governance, solvency and conduct.[web:420][web:422]
Intermediary StructureInsurance agents act for insurers under authorisation agreements. Insurance brokers act in the interest of the insurance applicant and provide intermediary services between applicant and insurer for a commission.[web:415][web:413]
Foreign Investment LogicForeign investment caps in the insurance sector have been removed. Foreign investors may establish wholly foreign-owned insurers, joint ventures or branches, subject to licensing and the applicable foreign-funded insurer rules.[web:420][web:422]
Language ExpectationChinese is central to local policy wording, regulatory filings, claims communication and contractual interpretation. English-language global programme documents should not be assumed to replace Chinese local policy and compliance requirements.

Applicable Legislation

Commercial insurance in mainland China is governed principally by the Insurance Law of the People’s Republic of China, the PRC Civil Code, the PRC Maritime Law for marine insurance and NFRA regulations. A draft revision to the Insurance Law was released for public consultation in September 2026; it should not be treated as the operative law until enacted.[web:423][web:426]

Process Flow

There is no single mandatory commercial-insurance placement sequence in China. The appropriate route depends on corporate risk, insurer licensing, broker or agency model, Chinese local policy requirements, compulsory insurance, reinsurance structure and whether Chinese local cover is part of a multinational programme. Most placements nevertheless follow a recognisable commercial cycle.

1. Define Risk and ObjectivesIdentify assets, operations, contracts, supply-chain dependencies, product exposure, liabilities, claims history, regulatory obligations and retained-risk objectives.
2. Confirm Placement RouteDetermine whether cover will be placed directly, through a licensed insurance broker acting for the applicant, through an insurer-appointed agency or within a multinational programme.
3. Verify Insurer and Intermediary StatusConfirm NFRA licence and relevant business scope for the insurer, broker or agency; verify local regulatory standing before placement activity begins.
4. Classify Local Policy and Cross-Border RelevanceIdentify Chinese risk location, local policyholder, compulsory cover, foreign insurer/reinsurer role, regulatory reporting and local claims requirements.
5. Prepare Underwriting SubmissionCompile accurate information on operations, assets, revenue, claims, contracts, risk controls, insured entities and requested limits.
6. Market and Negotiate TermsApproach suitable licensed insurers and negotiate premium, capacity, limits, deductibles, exclusions, conditions, endorsements, territory and claims arrangements.
7. Bind and Document CoverConfirm acceptance and receive Chinese policy wording, schedule, certificates, compulsory-insurance evidence and applicable local documents.
8. Administer and Manage ClaimsManage changes in risk, certificates, endorsements, claims notification, loss mitigation, evidence, regulatory reporting and insurer/broker correspondence.
9. Review and RenewReassess exposure, claims, contracts, local compliance, reinsurance support, insurer market options and programme design before renewal.

Decision Tree

The appropriate China placement route depends on insurer/broker licensing, Chinese legal entity and risk location, local policy requirements, compulsory insurance and reinsurance or multinational programme design. Commercial insurance should be structured from these market-access and operational facts rather than from premium alone.

Will a broker or agency arrange the policy?If yes, determine whether the entity acts as an applicant-side broker or insurer-side agency and verify it has the appropriate NFRA licence.[web:415][web:420]
Is the insurer authorised to write Chinese insurance business?If yes, confirm it holds the relevant NFRA insurance business licence. Unlicensed foreign insurers are not permitted to conduct insurance business in China.[web:420][web:422]
Will foreign reinsurance support the placement?If yes, confirm that the Chinese cedent and foreign reinsurer meet the applicable registration, financial strength, credit-rating and reporting requirements.[web:420][web:422]
Does the Chinese business require compulsory insurance?If yes, identify the relevant statutory, sectoral, motor, construction, employer or environmental insurance requirement and integrate it with voluntary corporate cover.
Is the China entity part of a multinational programme?If yes, identify local insured entities, policyholder, Chinese policy language, insurer licence, claims route, tax, data and master-policy interaction.
Decision logic: First identify the mainland Chinese legal entity, risk, compulsory insurance and market-access route. Then verify NFRA insurer and intermediary authority. Only after these foundations are clear should the company finalise policy wording, premium, reinsurance and multinational programme coordination.

Timeline

Commercial insurance should operate as a planned risk-management cycle rather than a last-minute annual purchase. Timing is driven by policy expiry, construction and production schedules, insurer underwriting lead time, product and supply-chain exposure, claims history, financing and tender deadlines, regulatory documentation and coordination of China local policies with global master cover.

Cross-Border Relevance

China is a major global manufacturing, trading and insurance market, but it is outside the EU/EEA passporting regime. Cross-border commercial insurance must therefore be structured through Chinese NFRA licensing, local policyholder/risk analysis and any permitted reinsurance framework. Foreign master policies, captives and overseas brokers require specific local analysis; they cannot be assumed to create a legally effective direct-insurance solution for mainland Chinese risk.

RecognitionCommercial insurance is not a separately licensed professional title. The material questions are whether the insurer, broker or agency holds the appropriate NFRA licence and whether the placement is structured through a permitted Chinese direct-insurance and reinsurance route.
Foreign CompaniesUnlicensed foreign insurers are not permitted to conduct insurance business in China. Foreign investors may establish wholly foreign-owned insurers, joint ventures or branches under the applicable foreign-funded insurance company regime and NFRA licensing process.[web:420][web:422]
Language ConsiderationsChinese policy wording, certificates, regulatory filings, claims communication and contract interpretation are central for mainland Chinese operations. English global programme documents should be treated as complementary rather than a substitute for Chinese local policy documentation.
International RulesChina’s market-access regime is domestic and NFRA-led, not EU/EEA passport-based. Foreign reinsurers may accept cessions from Chinese cedents only if the relevant strength, rating and registration requirements are met.[web:420][web:422]
Practical ConsiderationsConfirm Chinese insured entities, local policyholder, NFRA insurer licence, broker/agency licence, business scope, compulsory insurance, policy language, tax, data/local reporting, claims payment and local/master/reinsurance architecture.
Typical RisksAssuming that a foreign master policy, overseas broker, captive, non-licensed insurer or English-only wording automatically provides legally permitted and operationally effective cover for mainland Chinese risk.

Operating Constraints & Risks

The principal operating risk is treating China commercial insurance as a generic international placement rather than a local NFRA licensing, policyholder, contract-language, data, reinsurance and claims-management process. The distinction between an applicant-side broker and an insurer-side agency must be clear, and foreign market access should be tested before a company relies on global coverage language.

Market-Access RiskUnlicensed foreign insurers are not permitted to conduct direct insurance business in China. Local NFRA authorisation and business scope must be verified.[web:420][web:422]
Intermediary Role RiskConfusing an insurance broker acting in the interest of the applicant with an agency acting on behalf of an insurer can obscure authority, representation, commission and client expectations.[web:415][web:413]
Foreign Reinsurance RiskForeign reinsurance supporting Chinese risk requires analysis of Chinese cedent duties, foreign reinsurer strength/rating and China Reinsurance Registration System requirements.[web:420][web:422]
Local Policy and Language RiskEnglish-only master policy language or foreign certificates can be insufficient for local Chinese policy issuance, claims administration, evidence and regulatory compliance.
Compulsory Insurance RiskFailure to identify China-specific statutory, sectoral, motor, construction, employer or environmental insurance can leave a business exposed or unable to satisfy regulatory and contractual requirements.
Programme-Coordination RiskChinese local policies, permitted reinsurance, captives and foreign master programmes can produce gaps or uncertain claims routes if entities, limits, language, data and documentation are not aligned.

Costs & Fees

There is no statutory tariff for commercial insurance placement in China. Premium is determined by the insurer’s underwriting assessment of risk. Broker and agency remuneration should be assessed in the context of the Insurance Law, NFRA licensing, representation model, applicable disclosure and conduct requirements, potential conflicts of interest and the professional service scope agreed with the corporate policyholder.

FAQ

Is commercial insurance a separately licensed professional function in China?No. Commercial insurance is a business service line. The Insurance Law and NFRA framework govern insurers, reinsurers, insurance agencies and brokers that conduct insurance activity in China.[web:412][web:420]
What is the difference between a Chinese insurance broker and insurance agent?An insurance broker acts in the interest of the insurance applicant and provides intermediary services between applicant and insurer. An insurance agent acts under an authorisation agreement with an insurer and transacts insurance business for the insurer.[web:415]
Must an insurance broker or agency be licensed in China?Yes. Insurance agencies and brokers must obtain the relevant NFRA insurance agency or broker licence to distribute insurance products in China.[web:420][web:422]
Can an unlicensed foreign insurer write commercial insurance in China?No. Unlicensed foreign insurers are not permitted to conduct insurance business in China. Foreign investors may establish authorised Chinese entities or branches under the applicable rules.[web:420][web:422]
Is the Insurance Law changing?NFRA released a draft revision of the Insurance Law for public consultation on 4 September 2026. It is a proposed reform, so the existing law and applicable NFRA rules remain operative unless and until amendments are enacted.[web:423][web:426]
